Buying a home in the US involves roughly seven distinct phases, each with its own paperwork, decisions, and deadlines. The entire journey — from getting pre-approved to handing over wire instructions — typically takes 60 to 90 days for a financed purchase, though cash offers can close in as little as 7 to 14 days. Knowing what comes next at each stage helps you move quickly when the right home appears.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Get pre-approved">
    Pre-approval is the first step, and it's worth doing before you even start browsing seriously. A lender reviews your income, assets, credit score, and debt load, then issues a letter stating the maximum loan amount they'll extend. Sellers won't consider an offer without it, and you don't want to fall in love with a $1.8M home when your approval tops out at $1.3M.

    **Typical timeline:** 2–7 business days for a thorough pre-approval. Same-day pre-qualifications are common, but they carry less weight — a full pre-approval requires verified income documents (W-2s, pay stubs, tax returns) and a hard credit pull.

    <Note>
      Pre-approval is not a loan commitment. Your final rate is locked only when you formally apply after an offer is accepted. Rates can move between pre-approval and lock — budget for that range.
    </Note>

    Get pre-approved at at least two lenders. Rates and fees vary enough that a second quote routinely saves $5,000–$15,000 over the life of the loan.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Define your criteria">

    Common criteria worth thinking through:

    - **Commute.** A 45-minute drive each way is 375 hours a year. Define the outer limit before you start.
    - **Schools.** Nestlyze uses attendance-boundary polygons (not "nearest school" approximations) to confirm which elementary, middle, and high school a specific address feeds into. A one-block difference can put a home in a different school district entirely.
    - **Budget.** Work backward from your total monthly comfort number — including property tax, insurance, HOA, and maintenance — not just the mortgage payment. The [costs explained](/buying-guide/costs-explained) guide has the full breakdown.
    - **Condition tolerance.** Are you willing to take on a fixer? An "as-is" listing might be $200K below comparable turnkey homes, but expect $30K+ in deferred repairs on top of the price.

  <Step title="Make an offer">
    Once a home passes your analysis and tour, your buyer's agent drafts the offer. The offer includes more than just a price — it specifies contingency periods, earnest money, a proposed close-of-escrow date, and any personal property inclusions.

    **Earnest money** is a good-faith deposit held in escrow when your offer is accepted. It's applied toward your down payment at closing. If you back out for a reason covered by a contingency, you get it back. If you walk for any other reason, you forfeit it.

    - Typical: 1–3% of purchase price
    - Hot Bay Area markets: buyers sometimes put up 3–5% to signal seriousness
    - Once contingencies are removed, the deposit is at risk — there is no reinstating them

    **Contingencies** protect you. The three standard ones are inspection, appraisal, and financing. In competitive markets, buyers sometimes waive one or more to win — but each waiver transfers real financial risk to you. See [offer strategy](/buying-guide/offer-strategy) for guidance on when waiving makes sense.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Inspections and due diligence">
    After offer acceptance, the clock starts on your contingency periods. A standard California contract gives you 17 days for inspections and 21 days for financing. Use every day.

    **Home inspection ($300–$600 for a typical Bay Area home):** A licensed inspector examines the structure, roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and visible foundation. They cannot see inside walls — a clean inspection report does not mean there are no hidden issues.

    Due diligence items to complete in this window:
    - General home inspection
    - Sewer lateral inspection (especially for homes built before 1980 — cast iron and Orangeburg pipes fail)
    - Roof inspection if the general inspector flagged concerns
    - Natural hazard disclosure review
    - HOA financial documents review (meeting minutes, reserve fund study, CC&Rs)
    - Title report review for liens or easements

    **Typical close-of-escrow:** 30–45 days from accepted offer for a financed purchase. Cash offers can close in 7–14 days. Financing complications (appraisal gaps, rate lock problems, last-minute lender issues) can stretch the timeline to 60+ days. Your contract specifies a close-of-escrow date; missing it can trigger daily per-diem penalties.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Closing">
    Closing day is the finish line — and also the day the largest financial transaction of your life completes in about an hour of signing.

    Before closing, your lender will issue a Closing Disclosure (CD) — the final itemized list of every dollar changing hands. You have the right to review it at least 3 business days before signing. Compare it line-by-line against the Loan Estimate you received at application.

    **Closing costs** run 2–5% of the purchase price on top of your down payment. On a $1.5M Bay Area home, expect $30,000–$75,000 in closing costs. They include:
    - Loan origination fees
    - Appraisal fee ($500–$900)
    - Title insurance
    - Escrow fees
    - Recording fees and transfer tax
    - Prepaid property tax and homeowners insurance
    - First year's HOA dues (if applicable)

    You'll wire the remaining cash to close 1–2 days before the signing appointment. Call the title company directly to confirm wire instructions — wire fraud targeting real estate transactions is common. Never rely on wiring instructions sent only by email.

    <Warning>
      Confirm wire instructions over the phone with the title company using a number from their official website — not from the email thread. Real estate wire fraud costs buyers an average of $300,000 per incident.
    </Warning>

    After signing and recording, you receive the keys. The purchase is final.
  </Step>
</Steps>
